# Product Traceability Is Mature. Workforce Traceability Is Not.

<ArticleInfographic src="/01-product-traceability-and-workforce-traceability-infographic.png" alt="Infographic 01: Product Traceability and Workforce Traceability" />

## The people behind production are often less visible than the products themselves.

Industries can increasingly trace products from origin through processing, distribution and sale. A shipment can be connected to a batch, a farm, a processor or a destination market. Records, certifications and provenance systems support market access, quality assurance and trust.

But a structural gap remains: the workforces that produce those goods are often much harder to understand as a connected system. A product pathway may be orderly on paper while the employment and contracting relationships behind it are fragmented, changing and held in separate places.

## A different kind of supply chain

Workforce relationships rarely follow a simple line. In agriculture and horticulture, for example, a production site may involve a producer, an employer, contractors, subcontractors, labour providers and seasonal or mobile workers. A worker may be recruited by one organisation, employed by another and working at a site governed by a third. Each participant may hold only part of the relevant information.

The result is not necessarily an absence of records. Agreements, payroll entries, identity checks and audit documents may all exist. The problem is that they do not always create connected visibility: a reliable way to understand the relationship between people, entities, sites and events when it matters.

## Three questions that remain difficult

1. **Who is connected to whom?** Which organisation is responsible for the employment, contracting or service relationship?
2. **Where is each worker attributed?** Which legal entity, site or labour arrangement does the worker belong to?
3. **Can an event be traced and contained?** Can a concern or breach be understood without assuming the entire system has failed?

When these questions cannot be answered, organisations reconstruct events after the fact. Reconstruction is slow, incomplete and dependent on whichever records survive or can be shared. It can also widen uncertainty to organisations and workers who were not involved.

## From documentation to infrastructure

Workforce traceability is not simply a larger document register. It requires connected relationships, attributable records, event-level visibility and accountable governance. That means recording the meaningful links between participants, preserving who has authority to make or verify a claim, and making authorised answers available for legitimate decisions.

This approach does not assume that one organisation should collect every underlying record. It creates a shared capacity to ask trusted questions across a network while respecting the control and obligations of the participants in it.

## A wider opportunity

The first Labour Transparency Project trial is grounded in New Zealand agriculture and horticulture, but the challenge is global. Any sector that relies on layered employment, contracting or labour-provision relationships can experience the same visibility gap. The next stage of supply-chain governance includes the people who make production possible.

> Products should not be more visible than the people and relationships that make them possible.
